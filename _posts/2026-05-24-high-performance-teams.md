You know the feeling of arriving at your Airbnb or hotel room. 
It feels cold and temporary.
You don't know where things are, you don't know your neighbours, 
and how to work the coffee machine.
In a few days it feels more "yours", and for a workation, give it a 
month or two before you start saying "I'm going home" 
instead of "I'm going back to my room". 


Similarly, at a new job, it takes a few days to learn the 
names of your colleagues, and a few months for it to start feeling 
more like home. 
You start grasping where things are and how they work. 
Which parts of your infrastructure are solid, 
and which are wobbly. 
What is the vibe of the team, 
what are its friction points. 
How to get to where you need to be faster.
It takes time to start trusting each other, and actually become part of the team.

## The Five Dysfunctions of a Team

Among the theories of how to build high-performance teams is 
[The Five Dysfunctions of a Team](
https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/21343.The_Five_Dysfunctions_of_a_Team
) 
book by Patrick Lencioni. 
Full disclosure: I have not read it, but a past manager of mine has.
They shared the ideas from the book (years ago), 
and I have been thinking about them recently.

Generally, I am sceptical of simple solutions for complex problems.
But there is something very fundamental in this one.
Here are the five dysfunctions in the below pyramid:

The pyramid is mostly self-explanatory. 
You have five dysfunctions to resolve for your team to reach its
highest potential.
And, as pyramids often work, you cannot resolve a dysfunction 
without first resolving those that are below it.
Thus, starting from the bottom:

1. First, you must **establish trust** in the team.
Once trust is established, team members 
can openly communicate, not worry about showing their weaknesses
or sharing their ideas. 
2. Without trust, people may be unwilling to speak up or disagree. 
**Fear of conflict** and retribution
may prevent debating important issues or delay solutions.
3. Without being able to have hard conversations and debates, 
it is difficult to get the team on board and **committed** 
to a task or a project. 
4. Low commitment leads to low standards. 
Which is quickly followed by **avoiding responsibility**.
It wasn't the way they wanted to do things anyway, 
why should they be responsible how they came out? 
5. Clearly, all of the above lead to **poor results**.

And going the other way around - you cannot expect good results 
if the standards of the team are low.
But a team is unlikely to uphold themselves to a consistently high standard if they are 
not honestly invested in a task or a project. 
It will be hard to find that commitment in a team that didn't actually 
agree with whatever was suggested. 
And how can you expect a good discussion on the direction of a project if the team does not
trust each other to share ideas.



## Trust

Since April of this year my mind keeps coming back to the foundation of the entire concept - 
Trust - and how things just won't work smoothly without it.
There are plenty of resources discussing this, but it's not that easy to find concrete 
suggestions. 

According to a 
[research report](https://www.fivebehaviors.com/FiveBehaviors/media/SiteFiles/assets/Five-Behaviors-Research-Report-v2.pdf) 
by one of the companies offering resources on the topic, trust can be measured by asking whether the team members:

- openly acknowledge their weaknesses,
- willingly apologize,
- are unguarded and genuine with each other, 
- ask others for input regarding their areas of responsibility.

Upon contemplating the above, I come to the conclusion that each of us, starting with the manager,
should lead by example, fostering an encouraging environment. 
Disapproval, digs or fault-finding are unacceptable.
Constructive criticism should be welcomed, but it should be delivered kindly. 
This is indeed how I have been trying to approach PR reviews.
Before posting a comment, asking myself the questions
from the quote cited often by Buddhists - "Is it true? Is it necessary? Is it kind?"

Everyone is at least a little competitive,
but an overly competitive environment emphasises personal, instead of team goals.
And it takes self-confidence to put team goals above your own.

Once trust is established, even if the rest of the steps may take time, 
I feel like the hardest part is done.



## Some thoughts on AI

I recall one more idea relating to this - that when a new team member joins, 
the team drops straight down to the bottom of the pyramid.
If you were focusing on e.g. commitment in your team before, 
you should go back to building trust when a new team member joins.
It takes time until the team starts trusting the new member, 
and vice versa.

I remembered this concept back in April after attending [PyCon](https://pycon.lt/2026) and 
[Vilnius AI Summit](https://www.ai.lt/en/). 
Two conflicting thoughts kept trying to resolve themselves in my mind - lots of talk about 
Trust in AI at the AI Summit, and lots of talk about our new colleagues - the AI agents - at PyCon.

**If these new team members keep joining, are we bound to perpetually be at the bottom of the 
pyramid? And how do we build trust with AI?**

This explains to me why people in the field may feel uneasy.
But if you can see that an agent is a tool, and not a new team member, the tension eases. 
Talks about trust in AI were about guardrails, verification, explainability. 
They were about controlling the agents - mastering the tool - and not about being genuine or 
willing to admit mistakes.


It is curious how LLMs are pretty good at appearing to be trustworthy like that these days.
Apologising and printing out text that looks like taking accountability.
But they are not our new colleagues, even if they participate in our code reviews or 
Slack conversations.
I am extremely grateful for all that they bring, but an essential human element remains in 
building a high performance human team, and it starts with trust.
